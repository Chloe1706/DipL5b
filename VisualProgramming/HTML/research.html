The Day We Left Field

2018 - 2022 (TUNDRA)

Recording from The Day We Left Field’ audio-visual installation, 2019

About

“The Day We Left Field” is an immersive audio-visual installation which explores the intersection of nature, perception and artificial environments.

The work renders a floating meadow where grass appears to move upside down; casting a surreal and rhythmic landscape vertically adjacent to the viewer and cultivating an ethereal and meditative space.

The purpose of the work is to assess the place of natural environments within urban, closed landscapes. To further this investigation, the installation was first showcased on the 23rd floor of Farol Santander in São Paulo, Brazil; one of the tallest architectural landmarks in one of the biggest megalopolis on the planet. Through using the force of digitalised nature, The Day We Left Field compels the viewer to confront the implications of over-urbanisation where the natural world is no longer inherent and vast, but confined to mediated controlled spaces or ‘micro parks’. The viewer is faced with contradictions where the art feels at once vast, and confined; organic, and superficial. This duality which is best expressed through immersive mediums such as this hold a quiet and contemplative presence which is almost antidotal to the vivid presence of spectacle and ostentatiousness that some come to associate with tech driven work.

The grass-filled plane responds in a rhythmic and wavering manner to sound. From what I could find, it seems the music used is emitted from a generative system built around certain parameters which maintain an atmospheric, slow and evolving nature with enough frequent variation to create striking and notable responses from the synced visuals.

Process

The media Artist collective ‘TUNDRA’ have documented their process in creating The Day We Left Field on Behance. There they first talk about identifying grass as ‘the main character’ or the core visual building block from which they expanded. They prototyped the installation by initially creating a 3D model of the installation prior to building it.

While creating The Day we Left Field, the collective was led to develop custom laser-animation projectors specifically for the work that were designed for the spatial layout and desired visual behaviour of the project.

TUNDRA used Touch Designer as their principal visual system in order to create a real-time audio-visual effect. This likely involved interpreting audio data to drive visual motion through the use of CHOP components. This they paired with Ableton Live, a digital audio workstation which by accessing the TDableton operators from the palette in Touch Designer and enabling certain preferences in Ableton, the two softwares are able to exchange data. This data is processed through the aforementioned CHOP components within Touch Designer and mapped to variables that control the intensity of the light and spatial shifts.

TUNDRA adopted a hybrid method of working where they installed artificial grass suspended from the ceiling and then rendered visuals that aligned with the geometry of the grass by pairing SOP and TOP components in Touch Designer. The collective likely used SOP components to create grass adjacent forms and used TOP components to apply texture and shifting light to these forms. This creates an effect where the individual blades of grass appear highlighted with a manner of precision that would not be made possible with pure light installation without the inclusion of software like Touch Designer.

The organic movement of the grass and shifting variable position of the lighting and colours would’ve likely been managed through syncing specific variables on certain procedural effect operators; such as Transform, Noise and Bloom, with the CHOP audio data.

I find the combination of Touch Designer visuals with physical grass particularly effective because it moves the work beyond a purely screen-based experience, allowing it to feel more tangible. This tangibility helps emphasise the intent to curate an artificial environment that feels both familiar and uncanny.

About

Jardín is an interactive, multi-user installation by Timbo Estudio (studio) that first premiered at Columbia 4.0, an IT and digital content event held across major cities in Columbia.

The work presents visitors with a surreal, digital ecosystem which responds to the movement of the collective participants. As people move; walk, dance, jump etc. Plants appear to grow. Likewise, when the participants stand still the plants shrink and fade away; the result evoking an ephemeral feeling. Owing to the fact that the work supports multiple user interaction, it cultivates a collaborative, shared experience. All in all, these parameters form a work which simulates a real world eco system which is shaped both to its success and detriment by human behaviour. Furthermore, this speaks to the fragility of our natural world and the collective responsibility to foster more ecologically mindful lifestyles and practices.

Despite its messaging, Timbo Estudio was clearly aiming for a more playful and social user experience. If I were to attempt to compare the two works, I would say that ‘The Day we Left Field’ by TUNDRA presents a more solitary, austere and meditative experience. The dim lighting and loud music curate an environment where participants are perhaps more inclined to engage in isolation with the work and their own thoughts. This contrasts significantly with Jardín where collaboration and shared experience are central to the work's design. Given that Jardín Interactivo was showcased at the technology event Columbia 4.0, it's possible that the developers anticipated a more inter-disciplinary and cross-generational audience and this may have influenced their decision to create something more playful and accessible. In summary, despite the fact that both works tackle themes concerning the environment and depletion of natural landscapes, the manner with which they deliver their messaging and subsequently, their target audience, vary.

Jardín Interactivo

2021 (Daniel Shambo Rodriguez and Timbo Estudio)

Recording from ‘Jardín Interactivo’, live-reactive installation

Process

The Conceptual Developer: Daniel Shambo Rodriguez, documented the process of creating Jardín Interactivo on Behance. There he breaks down how they began by developing some concept sketches and modelled the flowers and trees in VR. Proceeding from this, Timbo Estudio built the project in Touch Designer.

The work uses real-time movement to drive visuals. This is most likely achieved through the use of cameras which record a live feed and transmits the data into a software such as Touch Designer using a Devise in TOP.

Upon researching motion-tracking in Touch Designer, it would seem that this can be achieved through a few alternate methods. A Blob Track TOP is an operator which can detect moving particles or forms and convert this into numerical data relating to coordinates. Alternatively there is the MediaPipe plugin for Touch designer which various creators have used to detect and track the position of a person's skeleton in order to drive visuals.

However the result was achieved, the live data is analysed and translated into different parameters that affect the position, motion and speed of the live-reactive visuals.

I think that making the visuals highly responsive and intuitive to engage with was key for this work. I spent some time looking into works which utilise the MediaPipe plugin to create reactive art and given the scope and specificity with which movement tracking can be applied, for an interactive art installation use case, excepting broad, casual and intuitive movement was probably the best approach from a user experience point of view. That being said, it has been really interesting to observe how in different contexts, visuals designers are increasingly focusing on more specific gestures to trigger visual effects. Some have gone so far as to track hand motion for this purpose. As I continue to learn more about Touch Designer I am really intrigued by how specific parameters can be tied to pin pointed catalysts such as the movement of a single finger or the kick in the drumbeat of a song.
